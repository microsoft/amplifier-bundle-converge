An explainer for amplified information workers · about 12 minutes

You define what must be true. It builds toward it — with you, over time.

The sit-down companion to the six-minute deck.

Read this first

You write two short documents — a vision and a set of contracts — that say what must be true of your project. A long-running AI session then runs the work toward them, launching many short-lived sessions in parallel and bringing you only the decisions a person must make. Your word is what ratifies a change of direction, and the words you ratify are the words every session then obeys.

01Why now

One person now builds what recently took a team.

Leverage changed. Coordination did not.

AI does more of the work every month, and an amplified person — one human, well-leveraged — now produces what recently needed a whole team. That value is captured only by working above the level of tasks and code, and by collaborating there too.

Meanwhile teams move too fast, on efforts too large, for yesterday's coordination to keep pace. Standups, tickets and review queues were built for a slower shape of work and a smaller number of things in flight at once.

What “above the level of tasks” means

Not managing a queue of work. Deciding what must be true of the thing being built, and letting everything below that be worked out and managed for you. Converge is a way of working at that altitude.

02The roles

Three roles run the project, and two of them are AI.

You are the intent steward — the one role that cannot be delegated.

A manager session is a long-running AI session that runs a project's work on your behalf, for days or weeks. Worker sessions are many short-lived AI sessions, each taking one bounded piece of work in isolation and returning with proof.

You are neither. As intent steward you define where the project is going and what must be true of it, and you make the decisions only a person can make.

Why the split matters

The manager session holds the whole picture and never does the work itself. A worker session holds one piece and never sees the whole. Each lane is a separate, independent session in its own copy of the code — never a sub-agent tucked inside the manager session, where one crowded context could quietly become every decision.

one, long-running — runs the operation

03The words

You write two documents, and the direction is grown, not declared.

Nothing has to be right on day one.

A vision describes the project as it will be when it is right, written as though already true. Contracts are the specific promises it must keep — each short enough to read in a minute and precise enough to check against reality. Both are written for people; everything beneath them is worked out and managed for you.

Draft, then locked

A document begins as a draft. It becomes locked only when four things are true:

  1. It says what it means.
  2. It carries a real example of right and wrong.
  3. It can be checked against reality.
  4. You have read it and agreed.

A contract that meets the bar may be held unlocked on purpose while its neighbours mature. That is your call, and it is recorded.

Changing a locked contract

Only by written proposal backed by evidence — a cost actually paid, or a failure actually caught. Preference is not evidence. Refinements are cheap; reversals owe evidence first. And no one, person or AI, can edit a locked contract in place: the system refuses the edit rather than trusting everyone to remember.

draft→locked →evidenced proposal→your word

04The through-line

One text serves two audiences.

The words you ratify are the words every session then obeys.

The vision and contracts are also where people align with one another — at the altitude of promises, not code review. A teammate's proposal to change a contract and the manager session's proposal look and behave the same: what changes, the evidence for it, what does not change, your questions pinned to the paragraph.

Review it yourself, or have the manager session review it first and bring you its reading.

Four words end a proposal

ratified ratified with edits declined later

On a teammate's pull request, your questions and your answer post back to where the proposal came from, so the conversation stays where that person already is.

It reaches tools you did not choose

The same through-line reaches a colleague, or another tool's coding agent, through the repository itself: a contracts README, an addendum every coding agent reads, a pins file of hard facts, and a guard that runs before a push. What, how and why never depend on which tool someone brought.

05Your part

Your attention is spent only where you are irreplaceable.

Anything else that reaches you is a defect, and is treated as one.

Four things are yours: defining the direction; confirming the major decisions as they arise — the system proposes, you decide; taste and judgment, of which deciding what not to build is the clearest form; and the critical thinking that turns a proposal into a decision worth standing behind.

And exactly four kinds of call

Ratify

a change to the direction

Commit

Prioritise

Everything else is the system's job, visibly. That last word matters: the work that proceeds without you is not hidden from you.

06The operation

The manager session runs the whole operation, and waiting on you is never the bottleneck.

Its governing strategy is to maximise the useful work that proceeds without you.

It owns the queue. Work is derived, never invented: every item traces to the gap between a contract and what exists, or to feedback you gave, and names the contract it serves. It plans what to build next and in what order, and shows you that plan with its reasons.

Then it briefs and launches worker sessions in lanes, fills the lanes with work that cannot collide, and notices silence.

Never the bottleneck

Within the limits you set — objective, budget, lane count — it accounts for dependencies and collisions, fills spare capacity, and parks whatever needs your word while everything else keeps moving.

Done means seen working

Completion is judged by a check the manager session re-runs itself — never by a worker session's account, and never by a marker file. It then integrates, verifies, re-checks every contract, and writes you a brief in plain sentences for your return. Each piece of work ends one of three ways, and there is no silent fourth:

done, with proof

stuck, with the cause

needs your judgment

Feedback is signal, handled with care

You will react to work in progress at any point — a screenshot, a voice note, “still not working” — and that is welcome. It is never filed as a raw ticket. It is read against the contracts and the work already running, dated against the build you were looking at, merged with reports that share a cause, split when one note hides several, and enriched with the detail your note lacked. When feedback is really a change of direction, you get a decision to make rather than a ticket. “Still broken” reopens verification, not the tracker.

07The app

The app has two places and keeps no data of its own.

Whatever you can do in it, the manager session can do too.

Direction is the vision and contracts as one experience: what changed since you last read, whether each contract is kept, your questions on any paragraph, every proposal awaiting you.

Operation is the manager session at work: your return brief, the plan, what is running against what waits on you, lanes running against lanes intended, evidence you can inspect, your limits, a place to drop feedback, and every manager session you run.

The limits are the design

At most five things ever ask for your word at once. You can do exactly four things: answer with a word, raise or lower a priority, drop feedback, steer. The app holds no store of its own — it shows the project's own truth and nothing else, so there is no second version of the facts to reconcile.

Where it lives

It begins as a companion web page beside your project, on laptop or phone, with your conversation with the manager session still in your usual place. In time the whole experience, that conversation included, lives in the app.

08Boundaries

It is not a tracker, a dashboard, or one-shot magic.

Autonomy is not the point. Attention that is worth spending is.

×a terminal dashboard

×a notification stream

×a code-review tool

×one-shot magic

It also does not own your project

Converge carries the how: the rules, the AI roles, the manager session's practice, the contract check, the app. Your project owns the what: its vision, contracts, work and results. Converge ratifies nothing, invents no contract where none is needed, and makes none of the four calls for you.

Honest dependencies

It composes proven pieces rather than rebuilding them: a shared work queue with custody, the parallel-lanes practice, worktrees and a terminal multiplexer, a lean base. From a clean machine, one install brings them up and checks them — or says plainly what is missing. Where enforcement is not yet possible, it says so rather than pretend.

09Getting started

One line, onto the Amplifier CLI you already have.

Settle the vision first, then the contracts. Let the work derive itself.

amplifier bundle add \
  git+https://github.com/microsoft/amplifier-bundle-converge@main#subdirectory=behaviors/converge.yaml \
  --app

Your first week

  1. Write the vision in plain words, then read it aloud. If a sentence needs a diagram to survive, it is not settled yet.
  2. Turn the promises you actually care about into contracts, one at a time. Not every commitment deserves one.
  3. Lock them when they meet the four conditions — and leave the rest as drafts on purpose.
  4. From there the work derives itself, from the gap between what you promised and what exists.
